Why do bond prices fall when interest rates rise?

The question

Why does a bond you already own lose value when new bonds start paying more?

By Matthieu Théard17 November 20265 min read

Pre-publication draft · facts under founder review

Governments and companies borrow by selling bonds. The buyer lends money today; the borrower promises a fixed stream of interest payments and the return of the original sum on a set date. That fixed promise is exactly what makes bond prices move.

Anatomy of a bond

Take a simple government bond with a face value of £100, a coupon of 4% and ten years to maturity. Each year it pays £4. At the end of the tenth year it pays the final £4 and returns the £100.

None of those numbers ever change. What changes is the world around them.

The mechanism: a fixed promise in a moving market

Suppose that, the day after you buy the bond for £100, interest rates rise and newly issued ten-year bonds pay 5%. A new buyer can now get £5 a year for £100. Nobody will pay you £100 for a bond that pays only £4. To sell it, you must lower the price until its overall return matches the new market rate.

That overall return is the bond's yield. It combines the coupons with the gain or loss between the price paid today and the £100 received at the end. Price and yield are two views of the same thing: when one goes up, the other goes down.

The arithmetic

A bond's price is the value today of everything it will pay, with each future payment discounted at the market yield. For our 4%, ten-year bond:

market yield 3%  →  price £108.53   (the bond pays more than new bonds: it is worth more)
market yield 4%  →  price £100.00   (coupon equals the market rate)
market yield 5%  →  price  £92.28   (the bond pays less than new bonds: it is worth less)

Longer bonds move more

A one-point change in rates does not hit every bond equally. The further away a bond's payments are, the more each of them is affected by discounting, so long-dated bonds are far more sensitive. At a 5% market yield, a 4% bond with two years left is worth about £98.14. A 4% bond with thirty years left is worth about £84.63. Bond investors summarise this sensitivity with a measure called duration.

The chart shows the same 4% coupon at three maturities. All three are worth exactly £100 when the market rate is 4%. As rates move away from 4%, the thirty-year bond swings furthest in both directions.

Why riskier borrowers pay more

So far we have assumed the borrower will certainly pay. Real bonds carry a second risk: default. A company with uncertain profits, or a government with large debts and a fragile economy, must offer a higher yield than a safe government to persuade anyone to lend to it.

The extra yield over a safe benchmark is called the credit spread. It is the market's running estimate of how likely the borrower is to fail to pay, and how much lenders would lose if it did. Spreads move with fear. When investors grow nervous about a company, a sector or a country, the spread widens and the price of its bonds falls — even if official interest rates have not changed at all.

A bond's price therefore answers two questions at once: what is money worth over this period, and how far can this borrower be trusted? The first is set largely by central banks and inflation. The second is where most of the drama of sovereign debt crises takes place — a story for later lessons on this desk.

Holding to maturity is not the same as being safe

If a bond's issuer does not default, a holder who keeps it to the end still receives every coupon and the full £100. The fall in price is then not a cash loss but an opportunity cost: the money is locked into a 4% return while new bonds pay more.

The difference matters most for anyone who may need to sell before maturity. When central banks raised interest rates rapidly in 2022, the market value of long-dated bonds fell sharply. Silicon Valley Bank had invested much of its deposits in longer-term securities while yields were low. As depositors withdrew money, it sold $21 billion of securities and, on 8 March 2023, announced a $1.8 billion loss on the sale. The next day depositors pulled more than $40 billion, and on 10 March the bank was closed — a failure the Federal Reserve's own review traced to poorly managed interest-rate and liquidity risk.

Why this matters beyond bonds

Government bond yields are the benchmark for borrowing across an economy. Mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and the valuation of shares all lean on them. When you hear that "yields jumped", it means existing bonds lost value and new borrowing became more expensive — for governments, companies and households alike.

This lesson ignores default risk, taxes and inflation, and uses annual coupons for simplicity. Real bonds often pay twice a year, and yields on riskier borrowers include a premium for the chance they will not pay.

Words used here

Bond
A loan sold as a security: the borrower promises fixed payments and the return of the principal on a set date.
Coupon
The fixed interest payment a bond makes, usually stated as a percentage of its face value.
Yield
The return a buyer earns if they pay today’s price and hold the bond to maturity.
Credit spread
The extra yield a riskier borrower pays over a safe benchmark, reflecting the chance of default.
Duration
A measure of how sensitive a bond’s price is to a change in interest rates. Longer bonds have more of it.

The takeaway

A bond pays a fixed amount. When market rates rise, the only way its return can match new bonds is for its price to fall — and the longer the bond, the further it falls.
