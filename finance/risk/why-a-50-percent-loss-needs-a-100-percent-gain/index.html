Why does a 50% loss need a 100% gain to recover?

The question

If an investment falls by half, why is rising by half not enough to get back to where it started?

By Juan Montes17 November 20264 min read

Pre-publication draft · facts under founder review

Percentages feel symmetrical. Up 10%, down 10%: it sounds like a wash. It is not. A percentage is always taken from whatever amount you have at that moment, and after a loss you have less.

The mechanism: a moving base

Start with £10,000. Lose 50% and you have £5,000. To get back to £10,000, the £5,000 must grow by another £5,000, which is a gain of 100% on what is left.

The general rule is that to recover from a loss L, you need a gain of L ÷ (1 − L). Small losses barely feel it: a 10% fall needs an 11.1% rise. Large losses are punished by it: a 50% fall needs 100%, a 75% fall needs 300%, and a 90% fall needs 900%.

Volatility costs money even when it averages out

The same arithmetic means that swings themselves erode wealth. Suppose an investment rises 50% one year and falls 50% the next. The average of the two returns is zero. But £100 becomes £150, and then £75. You have lost a quarter of your money despite an "average" return of nothing.

This is why professionals distinguish the simple average of returns from the compound rate actually earned. The more volatile the path, the wider the gap between them.

What it looked like in practice

The arithmetic is not hypothetical. Between 9 October 2007 and 9 March 2009, the S&P 500 index of large American companies fell 56.8%, from a close of 1,565.15 to 676.53. To return to its old high, it then needed to rise by about 131%. It did not close above its 2007 record until 28 March 2013 (1,569.19), five and a half years after the peak.

The technology-heavy Nasdaq Composite fared worse after the dot-com bubble. From its closing high of 5,048.62 on 10 March 2000, it fell about 78%, to a close of 1,114.11 on 9 October 2002. Recovering that loss required a gain of more than 350%. The index did not set a new closing high until 23 April 2015, fifteen years after the peak.

Both figures are price indices, which exclude dividends; an investor who reinvested dividends would have recovered somewhat sooner. Individual shares, unlike broad indices, sometimes never recover at all.

Time is the hidden cost

A large loss does not only demand a large gain; it demands the time to earn it. An investment that grows at 7% a year takes a little over ten years to double. So recovering from a 50% fall at that pace takes roughly a decade: a decade in which the money is merely getting back to where it was.

Leverage makes it worse

Borrowing to invest magnifies every move. Put in £10,000 of your own money, borrow another £10,000, and invest £20,000. A 25% fall in the investment wipes out £5,000, half of your own money, before counting any interest. A market fall of a quarter has become a personal drawdown of a half. And if the lender asks for repayment or more collateral as prices fall, a leveraged investor can be forced to sell at the bottom and lose the chance to recover at all.

What follows from the arithmetic

  • Avoiding large losses matters more than capturing every gain. This is the logic behind capital preservation: the first job of risk management is to stay in the game.
  • Diversification helps because it limits depth. Spreading money across investments that do not all fall together tends to make drawdowns shallower, and shallow drawdowns are much cheaper to recover from. It does not remove risk, and in a crisis many assets can fall at once.
  • Leverage should be judged by the worst case, not the average. Borrowing turns survivable losses into permanent ones.

This lesson uses simple arithmetic and ignores costs, taxes, inflation and income such as dividends. It describes how losses and gains compound; it is not a recommendation about any investment or strategy.

Words used here

Drawdown
The fall from a previous high to a subsequent low, usually expressed as a percentage.
Compounding
Growth in which each period’s gain or loss is applied to the new, changed amount.
Leverage
Using borrowed money to increase the size of an investment, which magnifies both gains and losses.
Volatility
How widely and how often a price swings up and down.

The takeaway

Losses and gains compound on a changing base, so a fall of L needs a rise of L ÷ (1 − L) to recover. The deeper the loss, the steeper the climb back. That is why limiting large losses is the core of managing risk.
