Case File No. 0215 August 1971Period 1944–1973

1971: the night the dollar left gold

The question

Why did the United States stop exchanging dollars for gold — and why did the system built around that promise collapse within two years?

Under the Bretton Woods system, foreign governments could convert their dollars into US gold at $35 an ounce. As dollars accumulated abroad faster than gold in Fort Knox, the promise became impossible to keep for everyone at once. President Nixon suspended convertibility in August 1971. An attempt to rebuild fixed exchange rates at a new gold price failed, and by March 1973 the major currencies were floating — the system of fiat money and floating exchange rates that still governs the world economy.

Timeline

  1. 1934

    The United States fixes the official price of gold at $35 an ounce.

  2. Jul 1944

    44 countries meet at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to design the post-war monetary system.

  3. Sep 1949

    The US monetary gold stock peaks at about $24.6 billion — around 70% of the free world’s monetary gold.

  4. Dec 1958

    Major European currencies become convertible. Bretton Woods begins to operate as designed.

  5. 1960

    Robert Triffin publishes Gold and the Dollar Crisis. In October, a run on the London gold market pushes the price to $40.

  6. Nov 1961

    Central banks form the London Gold Pool to hold the market price near $35.

  7. Mar 1962

    The Federal Reserve opens its first currency swap line, with the Bank of France.

  8. 1964

    Dollars held by foreign monetary authorities now exceed the US gold stock.

  9. 1965

    US inflation begins to rise, eroding confidence in the $35 parity.

  10. Mar 1968

    The Gold Pool is disbanded. A two-tier market separates official gold from the private market.

  11. 15 Aug 1971

    Nixon closes the gold window, imposes a 90-day wage and price freeze and a 10% import surcharge.

  12. Dec 1971

    The Smithsonian Agreement realigns currencies and raises the official gold price to $38.

  13. Feb 1973

    The dollar is devalued by a further 10%; the official price later becomes $42.22.

  14. Mar 1973

    The major currencies float against the dollar. Bretton Woods is over.

Economic background

The architects of Bretton Woods wanted to avoid the chaos of the 1930s, when countries devalued against one another and trade collapsed. Their solution was a system of fixed but adjustable exchange rates. Each country pegged its currency to the US dollar. The dollar, in turn, was tied to gold: the United States undertook to exchange dollars held by foreign monetary authorities for gold at $35 an ounce. Private American citizens had been unable to redeem dollars for gold since 1933; this was a promise between governments.

The arrangement rested on American economic dominance. After the war the United States held about three-quarters of the world’s official gold reserves. Dollars were scarce abroad and in demand, because countries needed them to buy American goods and to hold as reserves. The system became fully operational only at the end of 1958, when the major European currencies became freely convertible.

The financial mechanism

The flaw was identified early, by the economist Robert Triffin. The world economy was growing, and it needed more reserves to support more trade. New gold production could not keep up. The gap was filled with dollars, which the rest of the world accumulated as the United States ran balance-of-payments deficits.

But every dollar held abroad was a potential claim on American gold. The more dollars foreigners held, the more the gold behind each one shrank. By 1959 the US gold stock merely equalled the country’s external dollar liabilities; by 1964 the dollars held by foreign monetary authorities alone exceeded it. The position resembled a bank whose deposits exceed its reserves: solvent only as long as depositors do not all ask for their money at once.

This is the Triffin dilemma. If the United States stopped supplying dollars, world trade would be starved of reserves. If it kept supplying them, confidence in their convertibility would eventually fail.

For a decade, policymakers bought time. The London Gold Pool sold gold to stop the market price rising above $35. The Federal Reserve’s swap lines gave foreign central banks temporary cover for dollars they did not want, so that they would not convert them into gold. Then, from 1965, US inflation began to rise, fed by deficits linked to the Vietnam War and domestic spending programmes, and accommodated by the Federal Reserve. A dollar losing purchasing power at home was harder to defend at a fixed price in gold abroad. After sterling’s devaluation in November 1967 added pressure, the Gold Pool collapsed in March 1968.

The evidence

The chart shows the stock of monetary gold held by the United States at the end of each year, valued at the official $35 an ounce — so its movements reflect gold physically leaving or arriving. It peaked at around $24.6 billion in September 1949. From 1958, as foreign central banks converted dollars, it fell almost every year. After March 1968, when the two-tier market separated official gold from the private market, the drain stopped.

Actors and institutions

  • Richard NixonPresident of the United States, who announced the suspension of convertibility on 15 August 1971.
  • John ConnallyUS Treasury Secretary, who led the American side of the negotiations that followed.
  • Arthur BurnsChairman of the Federal Reserve from 1970, present at the Camp David meetings of 13–15 August.
  • Paul VolckerUnder Secretary of the Treasury for Monetary Affairs, closely involved in preparing and negotiating the decision.
  • Robert TriffinEconomist whose 1960 book set out why a dollar-based gold system could not last.
  • Foreign central banks and the G-10The holders of dollar claims, whose requests to convert dollars into gold brought the crisis to a head, and who negotiated the Smithsonian realignment.

Consequences

The immediate decision of August 1971 was presented as temporary. The Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971 tried to restore fixed exchange rates around a devalued dollar, raising the official gold price to $38. It did not hold. Speculation against the dollar continued, a further 10% devaluation followed in February 1973, and within weeks the major currencies were floating.

Three consequences outlasted the crisis.

  • Fiat money became the norm. No major currency has since been convertible into gold. The value of money now rests on central banks and governments, not on a metal in a vault.
  • Exchange rates began to float. Prices between currencies have since been set largely in markets, with periodic intervention, rather than by agreement.
  • The dollar remained at the centre. Despite losing its gold anchor, the dollar stayed the world’s main reserve and trading currency — a position it still holds.

The decade that followed was one of high inflation in the United States and many other countries, which lasted into the early 1980s. Ending convertibility removed one external discipline on policy, but it was not the only cause: inflation had begun rising in 1965, and the oil shocks of the 1970s and the conduct of monetary policy played large parts. Historians continue to debate the weight of each.

Case summary

Date
15 August 1971
Period
1944–1973
System
Bretton Woods: currencies pegged to the dollar, the dollar convertible into gold for foreign monetary authorities at $35 an ounce.
Mechanism
Dollar claims abroad outgrew the US gold stock (the Triffin dilemma), while US inflation undermined the parity.
Outcome
Convertibility suspended in 1971; floating exchange rates by March 1973.

The takeaway

A fixed promise is only as strong as the reserves and policies behind it. When dollar claims abroad outgrew the gold to honour them and inflation weakened the dollar at home, convertibility at $35 became a question of when, not whether — and the world moved to the fiat, floating system it still uses.
