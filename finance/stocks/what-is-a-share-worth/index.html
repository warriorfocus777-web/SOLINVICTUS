What is a share actually worth?

The question

If a share is a slice of a company, why does its price move when nothing about the company has changed?

By Juan Montes17 November 20265 min read

Pre-publication draft · facts under founder review

Buy a share and you become a part-owner of a company. You do not own its buildings or its bank account directly. You own a claim: a right to a slice of whatever the company earns, after it has paid its staff, its suppliers, its lenders and its taxes.

That last part matters. Shareholders are paid last. Lenders receive fixed payments first; owners receive what is left: the residual. In good years that can be a great deal. In bad years it can be nothing at all.

What owners actually receive

Owners benefit from a company's profits in three main ways. The company can pay dividends. It can buy back its own shares, leaving each remaining share a larger slice. Or it can reinvest the profits in the business, in the hope of larger profits later. Shares also usually carry votes, which give owners a say over who runs the company.

So the value of a share comes down to a single question: how much cash will this business generate for its owners over its whole life, and what is that stream worth today?

The mechanism: the future, discounted

£100 promised in ten years is worth less than £100 now. You could invest today's £100 and have more in ten years; and the promise might not be kept. To compare money at different dates, investors apply a discount rate: the annual return they require for waiting and for taking the risk.

At a 4% discount rate, £100 received in ten years is worth about £67.56 today. At 8%, it is worth about £46.32. The further away the payment, the larger the gap: £100 in thirty years is worth about £30.83 at 4%, but only £9.94 at 8%.

A share is a long stream of such future payments. Its value is the sum of all of them, each discounted back to today. That is why a share price can move when nothing about the company has changed: if the rate investors use to discount the future goes up (because interest rates have risen, or because the world feels riskier) the same expected profits are worth less today.

A worked example

Imagine a company expected to pay £5 per share every year, indefinitely. If investors discount at 8%, the share is worth £5 ÷ 0.08 = £62.50. If the discount rate rises to 10%, the same share is worth £5 ÷ 0.10 = £50. The company is unchanged; the price falls 20%.

Now suppose next year's £5 payment is expected to grow by 3% a year after that. The standard growth formula divides the payment by the discount rate minus the growth rate. At 8%, that is £5 ÷ 0.05 = £100. At 10%, it is £5 ÷ 0.07 ≈ £71.43, a fall of about 29%. The more a company's value lies in the distant future, the more its price reacts to a change in the discount rate.

What moves the two inputs

In this model, every change in a share's value comes from one of two things: a change in what investors expect the company to earn, or a change in the rate at which they discount those earnings.

  • Expected profits move with news about the company (a new product, a lost customer, a change of management) and with news about its world: competitors, regulation, the health of the economy. Because profits stretch far into the future, a small change in the expected long-term growth rate can matter more than a large change in this year's results.
  • The discount rate has two parts. The first is the return available on safe assets such as government bonds, which moves with central bank policy and inflation. The second is the extra return investors demand for owning something risky, often called the equity risk premium. When investors are calm, that premium shrinks and prices rise; when they are frightened, it widens and prices fall.

This is why share prices often fall when interest rates rise, as bond prices do. A share, too, is a stream of future payments, only less certain, and with no maturity date.

Price is not the same as value

A share's price is simply the last price at which a buyer and a seller agreed to trade. It reflects millions of different estimates of future profits and of the right discount rate, constantly revised as news arrives. Prices can run ahead of any reasonable estimate of value, or fall far below it. The model above does not tell you what a share will do next. It tells you what the price is made of: expectations about the future, and the price of waiting for it.

This is also how stock markets allocate capital. When investors expect a company's future profits to be large, its shares become valuable, and it can raise money cheaply by selling new ones. When they do not, capital becomes expensive. Every share price is, in that sense, a vote on where the economy's resources should go.

The formulas here are deliberately simple. Professional valuation uses forecasts of cash flow rather than a fixed dividend, and small changes to the assumed growth or discount rate can change the answer a great deal. That sensitivity is itself the lesson: valuations are estimates, not facts.

Words used here

Equity
Ownership in a company. Shareholders are paid after everyone else the company owes.
Dividend
A share of profits paid out in cash to shareholders.
Discount rate
The annual return investors require to wait for money and bear risk. It converts future money into today’s value.
Equity risk premium
The extra return investors demand for owning shares instead of safe government bonds.
Present value
What a future payment is worth today, after discounting.

The takeaway

A share is a claim on future profits. Its value is those profits discounted back to today, so the price moves when expectations change, and when the price of waiting changes, even if the company does not.
