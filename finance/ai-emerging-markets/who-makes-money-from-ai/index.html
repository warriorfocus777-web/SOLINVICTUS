The AI economy: who actually makes money from artificial intelligence?

The question

When the world spends on artificial intelligence, where does the money land first?

By Matthieu Théard17 November 20265 min read

Pre-publication draft · facts under founder review

Artificial intelligence is usually discussed as a technology. This desk treats it as an industry: a chain of businesses that buy from and sell to one another, each hoping to keep part of the value that passes through it. The useful question is not "how clever is AI?" but "who is being paid, and by whom?"

Six layers

Simplifying a great deal, the AI economy has six layers. (The illustration above splits two of them, to show eight stages.) Goods and services move up the stack towards the people using AI; money flows the other way, from those users back down to the businesses that make it possible.

  1. Applications — the products people and companies use: assistants, coding tools, search, customer service, design software.
  2. Models — the large AI systems those products are built on, trained by a small number of specialised labs and large technology firms.
  3. Cloud platforms — the companies that rent out computing power, storage and networking by the hour.
  4. Data centres and energy — the buildings, cooling, power connections and electricity that the computing needs.
  5. Chips and accelerators — the processors, above all accelerators such as GPUs, along with memory and networking hardware.
  6. Fabrication, tools and minerals — the factories that manufacture chips, the machines inside those factories, and the materials they consume.

The mechanism: spending runs downhill

When a cloud provider decides to offer more AI computing, it must first build. It buys accelerators, servers and networking equipment, and it builds or leases data centres and secures electricity. That spending is capital expenditure, and it is recorded as revenue by the suppliers lower in the chain long before anyone knows whether the applications at the top will earn it back.

The clearest evidence is in the published accounts of the chip layer. NVIDIA, the largest designer of AI accelerators, reports a segment called Data Center. Its revenue was about $15 billion in the fiscal year to January 2023. Three years later, in the fiscal year to January 2026, it was $193.7 billion.

That growth is not a forecast. It is money that customers — chiefly cloud providers, AI developers and large enterprises — have already paid. It shows where the first wave of AI spending has landed.

Energy: the layer that cannot be skipped

Every layer above the data centre ultimately runs on electricity. The International Energy Agency estimates that data centres consumed around 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, about 1.5% of the world's electricity. In its central scenario, that figure roughly doubles to around 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, slightly more than Japan's total electricity consumption today, with AI the most important driver. Power, grid connections and generating capacity are therefore part of the AI value chain too — and a constraint on it.

Where emerging markets fit

The same IEA analysis shows how concentrated the industry is. In 2024, the United States accounted for about 45% of global data-centre electricity use, China for about 25% and Europe for about 15%. Everyone else — India, Brazil, Mexico, Southeast Asia, Africa, the Middle East and the rest — shared what remained.

Several emerging economies are now competing to host data centres, attract chip assembly and testing, and train local talent, often offering land, power and incentives. Some of these efforts may succeed. But hosting a data centre is not the same as owning the profits it generates, and an announced investment is not a completed one. This desk will track those efforts country by country, and will be careful to distinguish an opportunity from a demonstrated outcome.

Who has not yet been proven

The revenue at the bottom of the chain is visible. Whether the layers above it — the models and the applications — will generate profits large enough to justify the investment is a genuinely open question. The answer depends on how much people and businesses will pay for AI products, how quickly costs fall, and how competition between model developers plays out. Investors are, in effect, paying the chip and infrastructure layers today on the expectation that the top of the stack will pay them back tomorrow.

Four questions for any AI claim

Headlines about artificial intelligence often mix three different things: money that has been spent, money that has been promised, and value that someone expects to exist one day. Four questions help to separate them.

  1. Who is paying whom? Identify the buyer and the seller. Spending by one layer is revenue for the layer below it.
  2. Is it booked or promised? Revenue in audited accounts is a fact. An announced investment, a memorandum of understanding or a projected market size is not.
  3. Where is the margin? Revenue is not profit. A business can grow quickly while selling its product for less than it costs.
  4. Who owns the asset, and where? A data centre built in one country may be owned, operated and taxed somewhere else. For emerging economies, that question decides how much of the value stays at home.

Asked of the evidence above, the answers are clear for the chip layer and still open for most of the layers above it.

One company's results are not the whole industry. Chip manufacturers, memory makers, networking firms, equipment suppliers, utilities and construction companies have also benefited from AI spending. NVIDIA's fiscal year ends in late January, so its fiscal 2026 covers most of calendar 2025.

Words used here

Value chain
The sequence of businesses that turn raw inputs into a finished product, each adding — and capturing — part of the value.
Accelerator
A chip designed for the parallel arithmetic that training and running AI models requires, such as a GPU.
Capital expenditure
Spending on long-lived assets — buildings, equipment, chips — rather than day-to-day costs.
Hyperscaler
One of the very large cloud providers that operate data centres at global scale.

The takeaway

So far, the best-documented money in AI has been made by the infrastructure — chips first, then power and data centres. Whether models, applications and most emerging economies will earn comparable returns remains to be proven.
