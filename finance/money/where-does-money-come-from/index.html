Where does money come from?

The question

If the central bank prints the notes, why is most new money created by ordinary banks?

By Juan Montes17 November 20265 min read

Pre-publication draft · facts under founder review

Ask most people where money comes from and they will picture a printing press at the central bank. It is a reasonable guess, and it describes only a small part of the money we use.

In the United Kingdom, the Bank of England has estimated that around 97% of the money held by the public at the end of 2013 took the form of bank deposits. Notes and coins made up the remaining 3%. The proportions differ a little across countries and years, but the pattern holds in every modern economy: money is mostly a number in a bank account.

That raises a better question. If most money is deposits, who creates the deposits?

Three kinds of money

The Bank of England describes three types of money, each of which is a promise — an IOU — from one part of the economy to another.

  • Currency. Banknotes and coins. A banknote is a liability of the central bank; it is the only money most of us ever hold that the state issues directly.
  • Bank deposits. The balance in a current or savings account. A deposit is a promise by your bank to pay you, in cash or by transfer, when you ask.
  • Central bank reserves. Money that commercial banks keep in accounts at the central bank. Households never see it; banks use it to settle payments with each other.

Cash and deposits together are what economists call broad money: the money you can actually spend.

The mechanism: lending creates deposits

The common textbook story says that banks collect savers' deposits and lend them out. The Bank of England's 2014 explanation turns this around. In practice, when a bank makes a loan, it records two new entries on its balance sheet at the same moment: the loan, which the borrower owes the bank, and a matching deposit in the borrower's account, which the bank owes the borrower.

No one else's savings are handed over. The deposit is new money, created by an entry in a ledger. When the loan is repaid, the process runs in reverse and the money disappears.

A worked example

Amara takes a £200,000 mortgage to buy a flat. Her bank records a £200,000 loan as an asset and credits her account with a £200,000 deposit. She pays the seller, whose account is at another bank, so the deposit moves there. Across the banking system, deposits — and therefore broad money — have risen by £200,000. Over the following decades, as Amara repays, that money is gradually destroyed.

What stops banks creating unlimited money?

If lending creates money, the obvious worry is that banks could create as much as they like. Several forces hold them back.

  • Lending must be profitable. A bank only gains from a loan if the borrower can repay it. Households and firms also have to want to borrow at the price on offer.
  • Deposits leave. Newly created deposits are usually spent and move to other banks. To settle those payments a bank needs reserves, and to keep deposits it must pay competitive interest. Both cost money.
  • Regulation. Rules on capital and liquidity limit how much a bank can lend relative to its own resources.
  • The interest rate. The central bank sets the rate on reserves. That rate flows through to the price of loans across the economy, which shapes how much people want to borrow — and therefore how much money gets created.

This is why the Bank of England describes central banks as influencing money creation mainly through the price of borrowing, rather than by fixing the quantity of money. The familiar textbook idea of a fixed "money multiplier", in which reserves are mechanically multiplied into deposits, is not how the Bank describes the system working today.

And where do reserves come from?

Central bank reserves are created in a similar way, one level up. When a central bank lends to a commercial bank, or buys an asset from it, it credits that bank's reserve account. No vault is opened; a number is typed.

This is how quantitative easing works. During and after the 2008 crisis, and again in 2020, central banks including the Bank of England bought large quantities of government bonds. They paid by creating new reserves. When the seller was a pension fund or an insurer rather than a bank, the purchase also added a new deposit to that seller's bank account — so quantitative easing increased broad money directly, not only reserves.

The two layers are linked but distinct. Reserves let banks settle with one another and with the central bank. Deposits are what everyone else spends. The modern system runs on both, and in normal times it is the decisions of millions of borrowers and their banks — not the printing of notes — that determine how much money exists.

Why this matters

Once you see that money is created by lending, several things become easier to understand. Booms in credit — mortgages, corporate loans — are also booms in money. When banks become reluctant to lend, as in a financial crisis, money creation slows just when the economy needs it. And a change in the central bank's interest rate matters not because it prints or withdraws cash, but because it changes the incentive to borrow, everywhere, at once.

This lesson describes the mechanism the Bank of England set out for the UK in 2014. The broad principle applies to other banking systems; the details of regulation and central bank operations vary between countries and have evolved since, notably as central banks expanded their balance sheets through asset purchases.

Words used here

Broad money
The money households and firms can spend: cash plus bank deposits.
Central bank reserves
Accounts that commercial banks hold at the central bank, used to settle payments between banks.
Balance sheet
A list of what an institution owns (assets) and owes (liabilities).
Quantitative easing
Large-scale bond purchases by a central bank, paid for with newly created reserves.

The takeaway

Most money is a bank deposit, and banks create deposits when they lend. Repaying a loan destroys money. The central bank steers this mainly through the price of borrowing — the interest rate.
